MIH The problem
Starting point
A Qualitative Defect, Not a Missing Layer
MIH is a systemically derived, qualitative enamel defect affecting at least one first permanent molar, with or without incisor involvement.1 The enamel is present in normal volume but under-mineralized, which is why it looks opaque, feels sensitive, breaks down under load, and bonds badly.
What you are grading chairside EAPD criteria
Demarcated opacityWhite, creamy, yellow, or brown
Post-eruptive breakdownEnamel loss after eruption
Atypical restorationRestoration outside caries pattern
Atypical cariesCaries following the defect
Missing due to MIHExtracted for the defect
Examine at around eight years, when first permanent molars and incisors are erupted but destruction is still limited. Teeth should be clean and examined wet.2
Why these cases are hard The pediatric reality
  • Sensitivity and anesthesia. Hypersensitivity complicates isolation and profound anesthesia, so treatment tolerance shapes the plan as much as the lesion does.2
  • Color predicts fate. Yellow and brown opacities are more porous and progress to breakdown more often than white or creamy ones.5
  • Bacteria get in first. Bacteria have been demonstrated in dentinal tubules beneath apparently intact but hypomineralized enamel.6
  • Repeat treatment is the norm. Restorations placed on affected tissue fail and get replaced, so the first decision sets the restorative cycle for decades.16
Framing for this talk
Every option on the table answers a different question. Bleaching changes the surrounding enamel, microabrasion removes the outer enamel, infiltration fills the lesion body, and composite replaces what is gone. Choosing well means knowing which of those the lesion in front of you actually needs.
MIH The tissue
Why nothing behaves normally
The Lesion Starts Inside and Works Outward
Unlike caries, hypomineralization begins at the dentino-enamel junction. In mild cases the outer surface stays intact; in severe cases the full enamel thickness is affected. That single fact explains why surface treatments have a ceiling.4

The clinical translation
Porosity is not uniform, the lesion body is not uniformly accessible, and the outer surface is often the least affected part of the lesion. Expect variability in every treatment response, and build that expectation into the consent conversation.3,13

Four Options, One Question: How Deep Is It?
The options are not competitors. They are a ladder, and lesion depth decides which rung the case starts on and where it stops.

Option 01 Bleaching
Option 01  · No tissue removed
Bleaching: Change the Background, Not the Lesion
Bleaching does not treat the opacity. It lightens the enamel around it and oxidizes chromophores, which narrows the contrast the eye reads as a spot. That makes it a preparatory step rather than a definitive one.
Least invasiveReversible
How it is used in these cases Protocol from the case report
  • Agent and regimen. 10% carbamide peroxide in custom trays, 2 hours per day for 20 days, with the guardian supervising home use.18
  • Result in that case. Shade moved from A2 to B1 by visual assessment, and restorative work was delayed one month afterward.18
  • Why wait a month. Residual peroxide interferes with bonding, so any adhesive step follows the bleaching, not the other way around.18
  • Sequence logic. Doing it first also matters optically: once a lesion is infiltrated or restored, its color is fixed while the surrounding enamel is not.
The pediatric question Policy and practice
  • AAPD position. Bleaching should sit inside an individualized, comprehensive, sequenced treatment plan; full-arch cosmetic bleaching in the mixed dentition is discouraged, and home products used by young patients should be adult-supervised.7
  • European constraint. Products releasing more than 0.1% hydrogen peroxide are not to be used under age 18 in the EU, a concentration that is not clinically effective.8
  • What colleagues do. In an EAPD survey of 110 members across 24 countries, 68% did not provide vital bleaching for children; among those who did, 72% used home bleaching, most with 10% carbamide peroxide, most commonly for two weeks, usually after the permanent dentition was established.8
  • The trade-off named. Where bleaching was declined, the alternatives offered were generally more destructive and required more maintenance.8
Discussion point
If the least invasive option in the ladder is the one most restricted by regulation, the ladder starts one rung higher and the tissue cost of the whole plan goes up. Worth asking the room how they handle this in their jurisdiction.7,8
Option 02 Microabrasion
Option 02  · A thin layer removed
Microabrasion: Excellent at the Surface, Blind Below It
A hydrochloric acid and silicon carbide slurry, applied with a rubber cup at low speed, erodes and abrades simultaneously. It removes superficial discoloration and regularizes the surface, and it does so at a cost of enamel that does not come back.
Irreversible, superficialLong clinical track record
What it is good for Indications supported in these cases
  • Superficial intrinsic stain. The classic indication, with follow-up reported over 18 years of clinical use.9
  • Post-debonding surfaces. Removes residual bonding resin and the white remineralized stain around it, and smooths the enamel afterward.10,18
  • Technique in the case. Macroabrasion first with a super-fine tapered diamond under copious water, then the microabrasive product with its dedicated rubber cup at low speed.18
  • Aftercare. Neutral fluoride afterward, and saliva restores surface properties over time.9
Where it stops The ceiling, stated plainly
  • It is a surface treatment. Enamel microabrasion removes only a thin superficial layer, so a lesion that reaches the dentino-enamel junction is out of range by definition.4,9
  • It does not open the lesion for resin. In vitro, microabrasion pre-treatment produced the shallowest infiltrant penetration of any group tested, 48.7 µm against 178.2 µm with no pre-treatment.13
  • It declares failure late. In the case report, incisal stains on two teeth were still present two weeks after microabrasion because they lay deeper than the technique could reach, and both were converted to direct restorations.18
Practice point
Build the two-week reassessment into the plan and consent. It is not a complication, it is how a lesion tells you it is deeper than the surface.18
Option 03 Resin infiltration
Option 03  · Microinvasive, fills rather than removes
Infiltration: The Only Option That Is Esthetic and Structural
Hydrochloric acid opens the surface, ethanol desiccates, and a low-viscosity resin is drawn into the porosities. Matching the refractive index of the lesion to enamel reduces scattering, and filling the pores reinforces the tissue. Both effects have clinical data behind them.

Reading ΔE in these papers One slide of colour science, then we move on
  • What it is. The distance between two colors in a three-axis space, here between the opacity and the adjacent sound enamel. Smaller means better matched.
  • Two flavours. ΔE*ab weights lightness, red-green, and blue-yellow equally; ΔE00 reweights them to follow human vision, so the two are not interchangeable.19
  • The two numbers to hold. Half of observers can see a difference of ΔE00 0.8, and half still accept ΔE00 1.8.19
  • The common trap. The pooled ΔE of 3.08 is a reduction in the gap, not the gap that remains.11
Option 04 Resin composite
Option 04  · Affected tissue removed and replaced
Composite: Additive, But Only After You Subtract
When the opacity is deeper than the microinvasive options reach, or breakdown has already happened, the question becomes where to place the margin. The evidence says: in sound enamel.
Why margins go in sound tissue Adhesion evidence
FindingConsequence
Bond strengthSignificantly lower to MIH enamel than to sound enamel, whichever adhesive class is used15
Etch patternMuch less pronounced, and etching exposes porosities and voids that act as a weak link for the enamel-resin bond15

With survival equivalent, direct wins on chair time, cost, and repairability, which matters in a patient facing a lifetime of maintenance.17
And prevention does not stop
Preventive measures should begin as soon as MIH is diagnosed, so breakdown, caries, and pain do not push the tooth toward endodontic or surgical treatment later.2,18

The honest summary of the evidence base
Only one of these four has randomized clinical trial evidence for a hard structural outcome in MIH, and that is resin infiltration.12 The esthetic evidence across all four is dominated by case reports and small series, and the certainty of the pooled masking estimate is low.11,20
MIH Sequencing
Putting them in order
Start Low, Reassess, Escalate Only If Needed
Depth is read before treatment, not after. In the case report, the tip of an LED curing light was placed against the palatal surface and each stain viewed from the buccal, where dark and intense areas indicate deep stains.18
Step 01
Bleach

Lighten the background first, supervised at home, then wait before bonding anything. Nothing is removed and nothing is committed.7,18

Step 02
Abrade, then look again

One conservative pass for superficial stain. Reassess at two weeks with the tooth hydrated. Residual opacity means the lesion is deeper than abrasion reaches.18

Step 03
Infiltrate

For non-cavitated opacity that persists. Do it for structure even when masking is partial, since it cuts breakdown roughly threefold.11,12

Step 04
Restore

Deep opacity or breakdown. Remove affected tissue to sound margins, total-etch, direct composite, incremental layering.15,18

Two chairside rules from the case Worth repeating to residents
  • Judge esthetics on a hydrated tooth. On one lateral incisor the stain was obvious under rubber dam but disappeared once the tooth rehydrated in saliva, so no restoration was placed.18
  • Transilluminate before you commit. The curing light you already hold doubles as the depth-assessment tool.18
When to skip rungs Escalate immediately if
  • Breakdown is already present. The tissue is gone; restoration is the entry point, not the last resort.18
  • Brown opacity with cusp involvement in a 6 to 8 year old. Every predictor of failure is present at once.12
  • The child cannot tolerate staged care. Treatment burden is a legitimate clinical variable in this population.2,8
Case report Oper Dent 2020
All four options in one patient  · 17 years old, 18-month follow-up
Where the Ladder Actually Stopped
White, yellow, and brown stains on the buccal surfaces of the maxillary canines, laterals, and centrals, with creamy stains on the mandibular anteriors. History of low birth weight, severe anemia around age one, and chronic bronchitis from 18 months to 8 years with repeated amoxicillin and nebulizer use. Both mandibular first permanent molars had already been extracted at age ten.18
The restorative protocol as performed Total-etch, three-step, direct composite18
  1. Bleaching. 10% carbamide peroxide, 2 hours daily for 20 days; shade A2 to B1; restorations one month later.
  2. Chromatic mapping. Composite increments placed on the buccal surface before isolation to select dentin, enamel, and incisal shades.
  3. Removal. Rubber dam, then complete removal of hypomineralized stain with a spherical diamond bur under water irrigation.
  4. Etch. 35% phosphoric acid, 30 s on enamel and 15 s on dentin.
  5. Bond. Primer on dentin only, 15 s of air to evaporate solvent, unfilled adhesive over the whole preparation, cured 10 s with a polywave LED at 1,000 mW/cm².
  6. Layer. Incremental technique respecting original anatomy: B2 dentin, B1 dentin, iridescent blue at the incisal third, pearl neutral enamel last.
  7. Finish. Aluminium oxide discs, abrasive rubber discs, silicon carbide brush, diamond paste with a goat-hair brush.

Discussion
Five Questions for the Room
The evidence points one way and everyday practice often points another. These are the five gaps where I would rather hear your answer than give mine.
01 Can we still start with bleaching? In the EU, whitening products releasing more than 0.1% hydrogen peroxide cannot be used under age 18, and that concentration does not work. Skipping the gentlest step means starting at a more destructive one.8
02 Should we infiltrate lesions we know we cannot mask? Infiltration cut enamel breakdown from about 18% to 6% at 18 months, but completely masked only about one lesion in three. Is it a structural treatment first, with appearance as a bonus?11,12
03 Are we conditioning surfaces out of habit? No pre-treatment before infiltration has proven benefit, and in the lab microabrasion made the resin penetrate less, not more. What do you do before you infiltrate, and why?13
04 How much sound enamel is a good bond worth? Bonding improves when all affected enamel is removed, but that costs structure in a child who will have this tooth restored again and again. Where do you draw the line?15,16
05 What study would actually change what you do on Monday? Most of the esthetic evidence here is case reports and small series, and the pooled masking estimate carries low certainty. Tell me the outcome, the follow-up, and the comparison that would settle it for you.11,20
